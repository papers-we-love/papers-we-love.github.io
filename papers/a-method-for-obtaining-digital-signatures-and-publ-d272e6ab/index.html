A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems

  • Authors:
  • A. Shamir
  • L. Adleman
  • R.L. Rivest
Read the paper
Published: February 1978; Communications of the ACM, Volume 21, Number 2, pp. 120–126

📜 Abstract

An encryption method is presented with the novel property that publicly revealing an encryption key does not thereby reveal the corresponding decryption key. This has two important consequences: 1. Couriers or other secure means are not needed to transmit keys, since a message can be enciphered using an encryption key publicly revealed by the intended recipient. Only he can decipher the message, since only he knows the corresponding decryption key. 2. A message can be “signed” using a privately held decryption key. Anyone can verify this signature using the corresponding publicly revealed encryption key. Signatures cannot be forged, and a signer cannot later deny the validity of his signature. This has obvious applications in “electronic mail” and “electronic funds transfer” systems. A message is encrypted by representing it as a number M, raising M to a publicly specified power e, and then taking the remainder when the result is divided by the publicly specified product, n, of two large secret prime numbers p and q. Decryption is similar; only a different, secret, power d is used, where e · d ≡ 1 (mod (p − 1) · (q − 1)). The security of the system rests in part on the difficulty of factoring the published divisor, n.

✨ Summary

The paper introduces the RSA public-key cryptosystem. It defines a trapdoor one-way permutation based on modular exponentiation over a modulus formed by multiplying two secret large primes. The corresponding public and private exponents support encryption and decryption without prior private key exchange, and the same mathematical structure enables publicly verifiable digital signatures. The paper also discusses key distribution, message representation, signed-and-encrypted communication, implementation considerations, and the relationship between system security and integer factorization.

Its subsequent influence is directly visible in cryptographic standards. PKCS #1 specifies RSA cryptographic primitives, encryption schemes, signature schemes, and key representations for practical systems; its current IETF specification is RFC 8017. (people.csail.mit.edu) RSA digital signatures were also incorporated into NIST’s Digital Signature Standard; FIPS 186-5 specifies RSA signature generation and verification while directing implementations to RFC 8017. (tsapps.nist.gov) NIST further identifies RSA as a public-key algorithm used for key establishment and digital-signature generation and verification. (csrc.nist.gov) These standards provide concrete evidence that the paper’s core construction became a sustained basis for deployed cryptographic protocols and public-key infrastructure.
