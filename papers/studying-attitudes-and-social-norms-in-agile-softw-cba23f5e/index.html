Studying Attitudes and Social Norms in Agile Software Development

  • Authors:
  • Lucas Gren
Read the paper
Published: 2018, published in the Proceedings of the 19th International Conference on Agile Software Development (XP ’18 Companion), Porto, Portugal, May 21–25, 2018; arXiv version submitted April 5, 2019

📜 Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to review research on attitudes and social norms and connect it to the agile software development context. Furthermore, I propose additional theories from social psychology (mainly the theory of planned behavior and using the degree of internalization of social norms) that would most certainly be useful for further sense-making of human factors-related research on agile teams.

✨ Summary

The paper is a conceptual review that connects social-psychology research on attitudes and social norms with agile software-development teams. It argues that research on agile-team behavior should examine attitudes together with social norms and values rather than treating individual attitudes as sufficient explanations of behavior.

The paper presents the theory of planned behavior as a useful framework. In this model, attitude toward a specific behavior, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control shape behavioral intention, which in turn predicts behavior. Perceived behavioral control may also affect behavior directly. The paper emphasizes that measurements should target specific behaviors—for example, the intention to share issues during a daily stand-up—rather than broad proxies such as general willingness to communicate.

It reviews five functions of attitudes: value expression, knowledge access, ego defense, social adjustment, and utility. The relationship between attitudes and behavior is expected to be stronger when attitudes are accessible, stable, based on direct experience, and frequently expressed. The paper also reviews limited software-engineering applications of attitude research, including studies of planned behavior, organizational change, and software engineers’ work attitudes.

For social norms, the paper distinguishes descriptive norms, which concern what people commonly do, from injunctive or prescriptive norms, which concern what people ought to do. It argues that these categories often interact in agile teams and that the degree to which norms are internalized—such as introjected versus integrated norms—may be particularly important. The paper therefore recommends context-sensitive research and interventions, including observational studies, participatory mechanisms, internalization of agile values, and demonstrations of desired team behavior.

The main contribution is a research agenda rather than a new empirical finding: agile-software-engineering research should use established theories from social and organizational psychology, test attitudes and norms against concrete team behaviors, and involve researchers from both software engineering and psychology.
