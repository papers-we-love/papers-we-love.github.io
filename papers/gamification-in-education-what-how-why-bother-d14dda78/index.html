Gamification in Education: What, How, Why Bother?

  • Authors:
  • Jessica Hammer
  • Joey J. Lee
Read the paper
Published: 2011 (Academic Exchange Quarterly, 15(2), pages 1–5)

📜 Abstract

Today's schools face major problems around student motivation and engagement. Gamification, or the incorporation of game elements into non-game settings, provides an opportunity to help schools solve these difficult problems. However, if gamification is to be of use to schools, we must better understand what gamification is, how it functions, and why it might be useful. This article addresses all three questions – what, how, and why bother? – while exploring both the potential benefits and pitfalls of gamification.

✨ Summary

Summary

Lee and Hammer present gamification in education as the deliberate use of game mechanics, dynamics, frameworks, rules, emotional experiences, and social roles to address problems of student motivation and engagement. They distinguish meaningful gamification from simply adding points or badges to existing school practices, arguing that interventions should target clearly defined educational problems and be evaluated for sustained effectiveness.

The paper identifies three principal mechanisms. Cognitively, game-like systems can provide appropriately difficult challenges, actionable goals, multiple paths to success, and progressive mastery. Emotionally, rapid feedback and low-stakes experimentation can reframe failure as part of learning and support persistence. Socially, role-playing and public recognition can help students develop more positive identities as learners. The authors describe possible implementations ranging from individual classroom systems to school-wide models and a modular “game layer” spanning multiple activities.

The paper also emphasizes risks: excessive reliance on external rewards may weaken intrinsic motivation, gamification can consume teacher resources, and mandatory play may reproduce the constraints and disengagement of conventional schooling. Its central conclusion is that educational gamification is a tool rather than a universal solution and should be theory-driven, problem-specific, research-based, and empirically assessed.

Influence

Subsequent scholarship has used the paper as an early conceptual source for defining and framing educational gamification. A critical review of gamification in education cites Lee and Hammer in discussing the field’s unresolved theoretical and empirical questions, while a later scoping review identifies the paper as one of the recurring sources used to define gamification in educational research. (link.springer.com)

Later empirical and review studies also draw on the paper’s emphasis on motivation, feedback, failure, and behavior change. These include research on gamification-based teaching practices, a field experiment involving a gamified learning application, and a meta-analysis of educational gamification interventions. The evidence supports describing the paper as an influential framing and agenda-setting contribution, but does not by itself establish that the paper caused particular industry practices or educational outcomes. (sciencedirect.com)
